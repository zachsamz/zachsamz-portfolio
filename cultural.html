Cultural Engagement

Part One — Definition

What Is Cultural Engagement?

Cultural engagement is the ability to understand, interpret, and thoughtfully enter the diverse cultural contexts — including ministry sub-cultures, generational dynamics, gender-specific communities, and life-stage realities — that exist within and around the local church. For the campus pastor, cultural engagement means refusing to lead only the people who are most like him and most visible to him. It means cultivating genuine knowledge of and pastoral care for every population the church serves, including those whose experiences are far from his own. Cultural engagement is not cultural accommodation — it does not compromise the gospel to reach people. Rather, it insists that the gospel must be brought to people as they actually are, in the specific contexts they actually inhabit.

Part Two — Reflections

Reflective Statements

Overall Reflective Statement

For my competence in cultural engagement, I am submitting the Ministry Observation Journals for both the Nursery Ministry and Abide Women’s Ministry, which together document seven distinct pastoral interactions with two ministry populations I had not previously engaged with intentional pastoral focus. The nursery section documents four interactions, including an initial meeting with the Nursery Director, hands-on service during a Sunday morning, a mid-year follow-up, and a final check-in. The women’s ministry section documents two one-on-one conversations with ministry leaders and attendance at the Abide fall gathering. What I discovered in both cases was the same: rich, faithful, important ministry happening in spaces I had not been curious enough about. That discovery — and the pastoral correction it required — is the central learning of this competency area.

Learning Reflective Statement

As I developed and demonstrated cultural engagement through this year, I learned that pastoral leadership has a natural center of gravity — it pulls toward what is most visible, most vocal, and most like the pastor himself. Men’s ministry, preaching, elder training, leadership development: these are the spaces where I am comfortable, confident, and well-resourced. Nursery and women’s ministry are not. They involve people whose experiences are different from mine, who serve in contexts I have never occupied, and who face pastoral challenges I was not equipped to name before this year. What the observation journal forced me to do was slow down, sit with people, and ask questions before offering any answers. The most important thing I learned is not about nursery logistics or women’s ministry structure. It is about what happens to a community when its pastor is genuinely curious about every part of it — not just the parts he leads. The quality of pastoral care improves not because the pastor has more knowledge but because people in every corner of the ministry feel seen.

Lifelong Development Statement

As a result of the experiences documented by these artifacts, I will continue to develop in cultural engagement by making regular pastoral visits to every ministry context on the Sheboygan Campus a structural part of my annual leadership rhythm — not as an annual residency requirement but as a permanent pastoral discipline. I intend to spend time in the nursery, in women’s ministry, in the children’s ministry, and in whatever other contexts emerge, not because I am qualified to lead them but because the people who are leading them deserve to know that the campus pastor is paying attention. I also intend to advocate for better structural inclusion of these ministry leaders in campus leadership conversations. The nursery director and the women’s ministry lead should not be receiving information through the grapevine. That is a structural change I am committed to making.
