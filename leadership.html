Christian Leadership

Part One — Definition

What Is Christian Leadership?

Christian leadership is the Spirit-empowered ability to influence others toward God’s purposes through servant-hearted character, biblical wisdom, and sacrificial love. It is not positional authority but relational influence grounded in the example of Christ, who “came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many” (Matthew 20:28). Christian leaders lead from below, not above — they equip, empower, and pour out their lives for the flourishing of those they serve.

Part Two — Reflections

Reflective Statements

Overall Reflective Statement

For my competence in Christian leadership, I am submitting The Peacemaker chapter journal and the volunteer appreciation log, which together demonstrate what I learned, experienced, and practiced in the areas of conflict resolution and servant-hearted leadership among the volunteers and ministry partners of the Sheboygan Campus. The journal documents eleven weeks of sustained theological and personal engagement with Ken Sande’s framework, including specific applications in real ministry situations: a confrontational conversation with a withdrawing campus member, a reframing of the facility proposal process, and the intentional release of a conflict I had been carrying unnecessarily. The volunteer appreciation log documents twenty-two personal expressions of gratitude across seven months of ministry, covering every major ministry team on campus. What these two artifacts share is a conviction that Christlike leadership is not primarily about vision or strategy — it is about the daily, deliberate choice to see people, honor people, and serve people in the name of Christ.

Learning Reflective Statement

As I developed and demonstrated Christian leadership through this residency year, I learned that my natural leadership instincts — while often effective — can run ahead of the relational wisdom that servant leadership actually requires. Reading The Peacemaker exposed a pattern I had not named before: I tend to resolve conflict by moving quickly past it rather than through it. I prefer forward momentum to the slow, uncomfortable work of genuine reconciliation. Sande’s framework forced me to sit with conflict longer, to examine my own heart before addressing anyone else’s, and to trust that the discomfort of working through a difficult conversation is worth the relational depth that follows. Separately, the volunteer appreciation rhythm revealed how easily pastoral attention can default to those who are most visible. The nursery team and the backstage volunteers had been faithfully serving for months without a word from me. That was not intentional neglect — but unintentional neglect is still neglect. I learned that pastoral attentiveness to the unseen parts of the ministry is not a bonus; it is a basic obligation of the office.

Lifelong Development Statement

As a result of the experiences documented by these artifacts, I will continue to develop in Christian leadership by treating peacemaking as a discipline rather than a response. Conflict will come in ministry — it is not a question of whether but when. What I want to carry forward from this year is not just Sande’s framework but the habit of self-examination that his framework requires: checking my own motives before engaging, extending genuine forgiveness rather than strategic avoidance, and entering difficult conversations as a peacemaker rather than a problem-solver. On the appreciation side, I intend to make volunteer acknowledgment a structural part of my monthly ministry rhythm rather than something I do when I remember. I will maintain the log format developed this year and expand it to include more personal contact rather than written notes alone. The lesson is simple but demanding: faithful Christian leadership is sustained, deliberate, and deeply personal. It does not scale on autopilot.
