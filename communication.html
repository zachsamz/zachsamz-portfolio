Communication

Part One — Definition

What Is Communication?

Communication is the intentional, Spirit-dependent ability to convey truth clearly, compellingly, and faithfully across a variety of contexts and relationships. In the ministry context, communication encompasses preaching and teaching, pastoral listening and counseling, written and verbal leadership communication, and the capacity to listen as well as to speak. Effective Christian communication does not merely transmit information; it forms people — it shapes how they see God, themselves, and the world in light of the gospel. At its highest expression, ministry communication is the Holy Spirit using human words to accomplish divine purposes.

Part Two — Reflections

Reflective Statements

Overall Reflective Statement

For my competence in communication, I am submitting the sermon listening log and the four sermon feedback forms, which demonstrate what I learned, experienced, and accomplished in the development of my preaching and my capacity to listen to and learn from other communicators. The listening log represents thirty-three weeks of consistent engagement with preaching — my own sermons reviewed by recording, and selected recordings from preachers I deeply respect, with a heavy emphasis on John MacArthur. The feedback forms document four sermons evaluated over the course of the residency year, tracking specific growth from September 2025 through March 2026. Together, these artifacts tell a coherent developmental story: a preacher who began the year with real strengths and identifiable weaknesses, and who engaged seriously with feedback to address those weaknesses over time.

Learning Reflective Statement

As I developed and demonstrated communication competency through this year, I learned that growth in preaching requires two things I had undervalued before: sustained listening and structured accountability. The sermon listening log was, at first, a discipline I kept because the goal required it. By November, it had become something I genuinely looked forward to. The practice of sitting with a sermon — summarizing the argument, isolating the application, asking what I would have done differently — trained me to think about preaching with a precision I did not have before. The feedback forms taught me something different: that I had blind spots I could not see on my own. I did not know my conclusions were weak until an evaluator named it. I did not know my introductions were passive until it was pointed out twice. What structured feedback gave me was a mirror — an honest, specific, uncomfortable mirror. That kind of accountability is not natural in ministry culture, where pastors are often evaluated by emotional response rather than craft. I want more of it, not less.

Lifelong Development Statement

As a result of the experiences documented by these artifacts, I will continue to develop in communication by maintaining the listening discipline and seeking ongoing structured preaching feedback beyond the residency year. The sermon log format will continue — I am convinced that a preacher who does not listen carefully to other preachers will eventually become insular and stagnant. The feedback rhythm is harder to sustain without a formal accountability structure, but I intend to ask our senior pastor and an elder to evaluate me at least twice a year going forward. I will also carry forward the specific areas of growth identified this year: stronger introductions that create tension before they resolve it, better-prepared conclusions that send rather than summarize, and application that is specific enough to be immediately actionable. Preaching is a craft, and crafts require lifelong, deliberate practice. I have not arrived. I have begun to understand what it means to be serious about it.
